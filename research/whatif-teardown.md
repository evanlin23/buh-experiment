## Why it worked

1. **The premise is famous before you click.** Everyone has aged, and most people know Benjamin Button. "What if you aged backwards?" needs no explanation, and the video asks it out loud on frame 1. This matches what the Shorts research found (`research/niche-analysis.md`): the topic picks the hit, and pre-known ideas win.

2. **It's a game with one rule, and the viewer plays it.** "Body runs backwards, mind runs forward" is stated in eight words and drawn as a two-number counter. Within seconds the viewer works out that the two meet at 40. The video then makes them wait two minutes for it. When Sam "does the math on a napkin" at 2:23, he is confirming the viewer's own prediction. That reward is the strongest retention device in the video.

3. **Visible progress, so nobody bails in the middle.** "Age X. Body Y." comes back 11 times, about every 27 s. The viewer always knows how far along they are, and that it ends at 80. It works like chapter markers that can't be skipped.

4. **It promises a science quirk and delivers a life.** The first third is comedy: walking stick in kindergarten, reading glasses before reading, free coffee. One sentence ("Then, the lines cross") turns it into a tragedy about outliving your wife and being cared for by your daughter. Viewers get a range of emotion they didn't expect from the thumbnail. Range drives finishing, rewatching and sharing.

5. **One running gag that escalates: mistaken identity.** The same joke comes back five times, each time with higher stakes:

   | Age | Mistaken for | Tone |
   |---|---|---|
   | 5 | "somebody's grandpa" | funny |
   | 20 | the professor | funny |
   | 50 | Mia's son ("is she your mom?") | bittersweet |
   | 60 | Lily's classmate | funny again (relief) |
   | 70 | a child ("where are your parents?") | tragic |

   That gives the story a spine without a plot.

6. **Every plant pays off.** "Hi, little one" opens and closes the story. The sticker wheelchair (age 10) comes back as Mia's sticker cane (age 70). Sam reads Lily bedtime stories, then Lily reads them back to him. "He doesn't believe you. Nobody does." (0:49) comes back as "The nurse doesn't understand. Mia does. She always did." (4:02). Payoffs like these are what make people say "I cried" in the comments.

7. **The writing is radically short.** The median sentence is 5 words and a third are 3 or fewer. Numbers carry the emotion ("She's 71. You're 71. Your body is 9."). There are no adjectives that don't draw something. At 146 wpm with no gap over 1.2 s, nothing is ever on screen without a voice telling you why.

8. **The science is placed, not front-loaded.** One insert at 31% justifies the rule (telomeres). The full "what's real" section comes only after the emotional climax, when the viewer wants a way out of the sadness. It gives sharers an "I learned something" reason to send it on.

9. **The CTA is a dilemma, not a plea.** "Would you?" has no right answer, so people argue in the comments. "Which what-if next?" turns the comments into a topic backlog and tells the algorithm this is a series.

10. **The production supports the structure.**
    - The story world is warm: sunset windows, lamp cones, rim-lit silhouettes.
    - The science is cool: a navy grid with neon line art. Switching between the two resets attention.
    - Each character keeps one shirt colour for life (Sam green, Mia red, Lily purple), so they're recognisable at any age.
    - Kinetic type puts the key word of each line on screen.

### What they did less well (our openings)

- **54% of frames are static**, with holds up to 6.7 s. On a phone, a still frame plus voice-over reads like a slideshow.
- **The characters are generic**: black stick figures with dot eyes. They're readable but have no texture or brand. A viewer couldn't tell this channel from five others in a thumbnail grid.
- **The letterbox wastes about 11% of the frame**, and is worse on mobile.
- **The science section is a list**: four facts in 52 s, each a separate diagram. It's accurate but flat compared with the story.

## The reusable script template

Use this for every what-if. The timings are for a 6-minute cut. Scale them for 4–8 minutes.

| Beat | Time | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Title line | 0:00 | The title question is the first words spoken |
| 2. Absurd concrete image | 0:02 | One sentence that makes the premise physical |
| 3. Sensory triad | 0:05 | Three nouns, each drawn |
| 4. Comic reaction + **plant** | 0:08 | Someone reacts. Plant a line or object that will return at the climax |
| 5. Name + rule + counter | 0:25 | Name the protagonist; state the rule in under 10 words; show the counter that tracks it |
| 6. Restate title | 0:33 | "So, what if…?" |
| 7. Act 1, comedy chapters | 0:36–2:00 | Counter beat every 20–30 s; 2–4 gags per chapter; one reversal line per chapter; seed the running gag |
| 8. Science insert #1 | ~30% | The real mechanism behind the rule; one metaphor; open the "what's real?" loop |
| 9. Midpoint payoff | ~40% | The moment the viewer predicted from the rule arrives; the happiest beat |
| 10. The turn | ~45% | One sentence that flips comedy to cost |
| 11. Act 2, the cost | 45–80% | The running gag comes back with higher stakes; state the thesis line around 55%; callbacks land |
| 12. Climax bookend | ~80% | The plant from beat 4 returns with a new meaning |
| 13. What's real | 80–95% | 3–4 real facts, extreme case first, ending on hope |
| 14. Dilemma CTA | last 15 s | "Would you?" + "which what-if next?" + subscribe |

**Line rules:**

- Median sentence 5 words.
- At most 1 s of silence in the voice-over.
- Write in the second person.
- Numbers on their own as sentences ("She's 71.").
- Each chapter ends on a reversal or a 3-word line.

**Topic test: a what-if is worth making only if it has all of these:**

- a pre-known premise;
- a one-line rule that can be drawn as a counter;
- a predictable crossing point the viewer can work out;
- a human cost that grows with time;
- real science for the ending.

## Fact check of their science section

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Progeria: old-age signs before 2; "a few hundred known cases" | Roughly right. The Progeria Research Foundation counts low hundreds of identified children at any time |
| *Turritopsis dohrnii* reverts its adult body to an earlier stage | Right. The medusa reverts through a cyst stage to a polyp |
| Yamanaka, 2006, four genes turn adult cells into stem cells, Nobel Prize | Right. The 2006 work was in mouse cells; the Nobel was 2012, shared with John Gurdon |
| 10 years later, a gentle version in progeroid mice; they "lived about a third longer" | Right. Ocampo et al., *Cell* 2016 (Salk): cyclic partial reprogramming extended median lifespan by about 30% |

The facts are accurate, which matters. A fact-check comment pinned under a viral video is a lasting tax on trust.
